# Stakeholder Insight Summary

Audience: hospital quality-operations / discharge-planning team lead (not a technical audience — business language only, same style as the OASIS-2 and Retail Sales writeups).

## Finding 1 — A history of frequent hospital use is the single clearest warning sign we found

Patients who had 3 or more inpatient stays and 2 or more ER visits in the year before this admission were readmitted within 30 days about 1 in 3 times — nearly three times the average readmission rate across all patients (11%). This isn't just "sicker patients being sicker": the two histories compound each other. Among patients with no prior inpatient stays, ER visit history barely moves the readmission rate at all. But among patients who already have 3 or more prior inpatient stays, additional ER visit history adds substantially more risk on top of that. Utilization history isn't one risk factor among many here — it's the strongest signal in the entire dataset, and unlike a diagnosis or lab result, it's already known the moment a patient is admitted. This also lines up with LACE, a discharge-risk tool already used in many hospitals, which weighs emergency visit history heavily too — so this isn't a new or unproven idea, it's confirmation of an established one, in this hospital's own patients.

## Finding 2 — Young adults with diabetes as their primary diagnosis are a distinct high-risk group, not just "diabetes patients in general"

Diabetes patients overall don't stand out much by age — except for one clear exception. Patients aged 20-30 admitted primarily for diabetes were readmitted at over 20%, compared to roughly 12-15% for diabetes patients at every other adult age. This isn't explained by medication load or anything else checked — it's specific to this age group and this diagnosis together. This age range is a recognized gap in diabetes care nationally: young adults are often transitioning from pediatric to adult-managed care, a period associated with higher rates of missed follow-up and treatment non-adherence. This data confirms that's a real, measurable pattern in this hospital's own patients, not just a general concern from the literature.

## Finding 3 — Long hospital stays only become a red flag for readmission when paired with a heavy medication load

On their own, a longer hospital stay and a high number of discharge medications are each mildly associated with higher readmission risk. But together they create the single highest-risk combination found anywhere in this analysis: patients staying 10 or more days who leave on 26 or more medications were readmitted at over 15% — well above patients with either factor alone. Shorter stays with many medications, or long stays with few medications, don't show this same effect. It's specifically the combination that matters, which points to a genuinely more medically complex group of patients, not simply a longer recovery.

What could you do with these findings
---
- Use hospital-utilization history (prior inpatient stays + prior ER visits) as a same-day discharge-planning flag. This information is already available at admission, so at-risk patients can be identified before discharge planning starts, rather than being noticed only after they're readmitted. (The accompanying dashboard's risk watchlist already surfaces this daily.)
- Build a dedicated young-adult diabetes transition protocol. For patients aged 20-30 admitted primarily for diabetes, add a diabetes educator consult and a structured follow-up appointment within 7 days of discharge, rather than relying on the same general diabetes discharge instructions used across all ages.
- Add a pharmacist-led medication reconciliation review specifically for patients with both a 10+ day stay and 26+ discharge medications, rather than a blanket review policy for all long-stay patients. This targets the actual highest-risk overlap instead of spreading review resources evenly across everyone.
- Extend that same medication reconciliation review to elderly patients (80+) on a heavy medication load, even when their stay wasn't long — see the additional finding below, this is a distinct risk pattern from the stay/medication combination above.

## Additional findings (supporting context)

These didn't make the top 3 — either the effect was smaller, or it's operational context rather than a readmission-risk driver — but they came out of the same analysis and are worth having on record.

**Elderly patients on many medications are a second, distinct high-risk group.** Separately from the long-stay/heavy-medication combination in Finding 3, medication count on its own becomes a much stronger risk factor as patients get older, independent of how long they stayed. Among patients under 60, having more medications makes little difference to readmission risk. But among patients aged 80-90, those on 26-30 medications were readmitted more than twice as often as those on only 1-5 medications (16.5% vs. 7.7%). This is a second, separate reason to prioritize medication reconciliation for elderly patients specifically, not just long-stay patients.

**Diagnosis type has a modest but real effect on readmission risk, on its own.** Looking at readmission rate by primary diagnosis category alone (not combined with age, as in Finding 2), diabetes-primary encounters carry the highest overall rate (13.1%), followed by injury (12.4%) and circulatory conditions (11.7%) — musculoskeletal diagnoses carry the lowest (9.5%). The spread here (about 3.5 percentage points top to bottom) is real but much smaller than the utilization or age-diabetes findings above — diagnosis type alone is a comparatively weak signal next to a patient's utilization history.

**Diagnosis type also affects how long patients stay, which matters for bed planning.** Separately from readmission risk, average length of stay varies by diagnosis: neoplasm (cancer) diagnoses have the longest average stay (5.3 days), musculoskeletal diagnoses the shortest (3.9 days). This is a resource-planning signal rather than a readmission-risk one — useful for anticipating bed demand by diagnosis mix, not for discharge planning specifically.

**Where a patient was admitted from shows a modest, partly data-quality-driven pattern.** Readmission rate varies somewhat by admission source (roughly 9.5% to 15.7% across sources with enough patients to measure reliably). However, a meaningful share of encounters (about 7%) have an admission source that isn't a real category — it's simply unrecorded or unmapped in the source data. This is shown on the dashboard for completeness but shouldn't be treated as a strong standalone finding without cleaner source data.

**A risk-scoring model was also built and is live in the dashboard.** Beyond the findings above (all based on direct rate comparisons), a predictive model was trained to score every current patient's individual readmission risk, not just their group's average rate. It correctly ranks higher-risk patients above lower-risk ones about 61% of the time better than chance would allow — a real but modest signal, consistent with how much of readmission risk this data alone can explain. Its top individual risk factors match the findings above almost exactly (prior inpatient visits, then prior ER visits, then length of stay, then medication count, in that order) — an independent confirmation that the same patterns show up whether you look at group averages or an individual-level model. This model powers the dashboard's daily risk watchlist referenced in the actions above.

## Draft one-paragraph summary (for README / portfolio site)

This project analyzes over 100,000 hospital encounters for diabetic patients to identify who is most likely to be readmitted within 30 days, and why. Using SQL (Azure SQL Database) for data modeling and analysis, Python for exploratory analysis and a predictive risk model, and Power BI for an interactive dashboard, the analysis found that prior hospital utilization (inpatient stays and ER visits) is the strongest and most compounding predictor of readmission risk — patients with extensive utilization histories are readmitted at nearly three times the average rate. Two further targeted findings — a diabetes-specific risk spike in young adults, and a long-stay/high-medication-load interaction — point toward specific, actionable interventions rather than a one-size-fits-all discharge policy. A logistic regression risk-scoring model and an accompanying Power BI dashboard operationalize these findings into a daily discharge-planning tool.
